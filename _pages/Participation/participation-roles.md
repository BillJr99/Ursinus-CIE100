## Rotating Discussion Roles

### Goals

- To understand the four rotating discussion roles and what each one contributes to a seminar
- To prepare specifically for the role you hold on a given day
- To experience discussion from more than one seat over the semester

A good seminar is a conversation, and somebody has to open it, somebody has to keep it honest, somebody has to keep it grounded in the text, and somebody has to knit it together.  To make those jobs visible, and to make sure participation takes more than one form, we rotate four structured roles through the section.  On any given discussion day you may be assigned one, and over the semester you will hold each of them more than once.  Knowing your role ahead of time tells you exactly what to prepare, and it gives you a way to contribute even on a day when the reading has left you unsure what you think.

#### Purpose

The roles exist for two reasons.  First, they spread the labor of a good discussion around, so it doesn't all land on whoever is most comfortable speaking.  Second, they let you practice the intellectual moves that strong discussion and strong writing are built from: framing a question, grounding a claim in evidence, pressing on an argument, and connecting ideas across texts.  Each role gives you a chance to rehearse one of those moves out loud, with low stakes, before you have to do it alone on the page.

#### The Four Roles

##### Facilitator

**What you do:** open the discussion and keep it moving.  Arrive with two or three real questions (the [Preparing for Discussion]({{ site.baseurl }}/Participation/PreparingForDiscussion) guide shows you how to grow a question from a quotation), invite specific people in, especially the quieter voices, and notice when the conversation has exhausted a thread and needs a new one.

**How to prepare:** write your questions in advance and sequence them from concrete ("what is actually happening in this passage?") to interpretive ("what does it mean?") to evaluative ("is it right? do we agree?").  Have one more question than you think you'll need.  You don't need the answers.  You need to keep the room asking.

##### Evidence Keeper

**What you do:** keep the discussion honest to the text.  When someone makes a claim, you are the one who asks, kindly, "where do you see that?" and then helps find the line.  When the conversation drifts into opinion that's come untethered from the reading, you bring it back to the page.

**How to prepare:** come with your anchor passages marked and their page numbers ready.  Pick three or four lines you expect the discussion will need, and be ready to read them aloud.  You aren't the fact police.  You are the person who makes sure we are arguing about *this text* and not about a half-remembered version of it.

##### Devil's Advocate

**What you do:** keep the discussion from agreeing too easily.  When the room converges, voice the strongest objection you can find, the counter-position a thoughtful dissenter would actually hold.  Steelman the view nobody in the room is defending.

**How to prepare:** use the *steelman the other side* exercise from the [Preparing for Discussion]({{ site.baseurl }}/Participation/PreparingForDiscussion) guide.  Write the best version of the argument against wherever you expect the class to land, and anchor it in at least one line from the text.  The one rule for this role is sincerity.  Your objection has to be one a careful reader could actually hold.

##### Connector

**What you do:** knit the conversation together and tie it to the wider course.  Listen for the moment two classmates are making the same point in different words, or making opposite points that clarify each other, and name it out loud.  Link the day's text to an earlier one on the syllabus and to the four Core Questions.

**How to prepare:** before class, name one connection between today's reading and something we have already read, and one way today's text speaks to a Core Question: *what should matter to me, how should we live together, how can we understand the world, what will I do?*  During class, keep a running note of who said what, so that near the end you can say "here is where we have been."

#### How Roles Rotate

Roles rotate so that everyone holds each one several times across the semester.  I post or announce the rotation, so you'll always know your assignment before the meeting.  On days with no formal role assignment, and in every role, the ordinary work of participation continues: everyone reads, everyone contributes, everyone listens.  The roles add structure on top of that shared responsibility; they do not replace it.

#### How Role Performance Counts

Serving in a role well is one of the clearest ways to earn both halves of your participation grade.  It is a meaningful contribution on the day, which is the daily half-point, and it demonstrates the quality dimensions (preparation, contribution, listening and building, and intellectual risk) that the [participation rubric]({{ site.baseurl }}/Participation/PreparingForDiscussion) rewards.  A facilitator who sequences good questions, an evidence keeper who grounds the room, a devil's advocate who steelmans in earnest, and a connector who ties the threads together are all doing what the rubric describes at its proficient level.
