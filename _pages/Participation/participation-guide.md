In a discussion seminar, your voice is part of the course material.  We build the section's thinking together, one meeting at a time, and that only works when people arrive ready to talk.  Speaking up in a room of sixteen people about a text you met three days ago is hard, but it is a skill you can practice, and this page gives you ways to practice it.  Please come back to it before each new discussion, so you're not improvising your way into the conversation.

## Purpose

A seminar works when people have done the reading and arrive with something to *do* with it.  This guide gives you a routine for preparing, a handful of exercises for the days when a text won't open, and a clear picture of how I assess participation, so that the grade rewards preparation and growth rather than how much airtime you take.

## How Participation Is Earned and Evaluated

This page is worth **10 points**, and you earn them through one thing: the **end-of-semester participation self-assessment** described below, which you submit through Canvas.  In that self-assessment, you look back at the routine on this page and show me how your preparation and participation grew.

The rest of the In-Class Participation grade, including the daily credit for contributing meaningfully and the rubric for quality of engagement, is described in [the syllabus]({{ site.baseurl }}/#participation); please read it there rather than relying on a summary here.  I define a meaningful contribution broadly on purpose: offering an idea or a question, building on a classmate's point, bringing in your pre-class writing, taking a discussion role, or contributing through whatever channel we are using that day.

Participation is a practice you get better at over the semester, and I grade it that way.  If the room is hard for you, the routine and exercises below are a way in, and my office hours are always open so we can make a plan together.  Please don't wait until it has become a problem.

## A Repeatable Routine for Preparing

Please run through this routine before each discussion.  It takes the reading you were going to do anyway and turns it into things to say.  It pairs with [How to Read Hard Texts in CIE]({{ site.baseurl }}/Participation/ReadingHardTexts), which helps you get *through* a text; this page helps you arrive ready to talk about it.

1.  **Read in passes, pencil in hand.**  Use the three-pass reading from the reading guide, and mark up the page as you go.
2.  **Mark two or three anchor passages.**  Find the moments that struck you, convinced you, confused you, or annoyed you.  These passages are your way into the conversation: with three of them marked, you will always have something to point to.
3.  **Annotate one passage for writing.**  Take a single anchor passage and answer, in a sentence each: *the thing that strikes me most about this is...*, *what I understand it to be saying is...*, and *how does this passage speak to each of the four Core Questions* (what should matter to me, how should we live together, how can we understand the world, what will I do)?  This is the course's own reading-to-write move, and the close reading you do here is the first step of your next essay.
4.  **Harvest three quotations into your commonplace book.**  Copy the lines that stopped you, each with its page number and one line on why.  A quotation you harvest in the moment comes with your reaction attached, and that reaction later becomes a discussion contribution or a piece of essay evidence with very little extra work.
5.  **Grow one question from a quotation.**  Take one harvested quotation and turn it into a real question, one that careful readers could disagree about, rather than a quiz question with a known answer.  A strong discussion question and a strong thesis are close cousins: the question opens ("*How does...?*") and the thesis closes ("*Because..., ...*").
6.  **Arrive with a position and a counter-position.**  Decide one thing you actually think about the reading and can defend by pointing at a line, and one argument you could imagine making against it.  With both in hand, you can start the conversation and keep it honest.

Please bring your annotation or your grown question to class.  It shows me that the reading happened, and it is the raw material a discussion runs on.

## Exercises to Prepare for Discussion

When the routine isn't enough, because a text is resisting you or because you want to sharpen a contribution before you make it, try one of these.  They are general, so you can use any of them on any reading on the syllabus.

- **The quarter-inch turn.**  Write your best observation about the text as a neutral sentence, then turn it into a question (so what? who would disagree?), then turn the question into a claim someone could argue with.  Bring whichever version is sharpest.  You'll use this move in every essay you write for me.
- **Point to the text.**  Take any opinion you hold about the reading and find the one line that most supports it, and then, which is harder, the one line that most complicates it.  An opinion tied to a specific line gives the discussion something to work with; an opinion without one is hard for anyone else to engage.
- **Argue the other side.**  Before class, write the strongest version of the view you disagree with, in a form its holder would endorse.  Either your disagreement gets sharper or you discover it was thinner than you thought, and both are good outcomes in a seminar.
- **Sighting in the wild.**  Find the reading's idea already loose in the culture, in a meme, a lyric, an advertisement, or a news story, and note what the borrower kept and what they dropped.  It is an easy way in, and it doubles as a commonplace-book entry.
- **Two texts in one room.**  Put the day's reading next to an earlier one on the syllabus and name one place they agree and one place they collide.  Connections across texts are where the four Core Questions come into focus, and they tend to move a discussion further than anything else.

## Ways to Contribute (UDL)

This section follows Universal Design for Learning (UDL), which asks a course to offer more than one way to engage and to show what you know.  Speaking first and often is one way to participate, and for some of you it's the natural one.  I've built the daily credit to honor several other ways as well:

- **Speak:** offer an idea, a reading of a passage, or a question.
- **Build:** respond to a classmate by name, extend their point, or press on it respectfully.
- **Ask:** a real question about the text often does more for a discussion than an answer does.
- **Take a role:** the rotating [discussion roles]({{ site.baseurl }}/Participation/DiscussionRoles) give you a defined job and a different way to contribute on a given day.
- **Write into the room:** when we use a shared document, a written thread, or exit notes, thoughtful writing there counts as fully as speaking.
- **Follow up:** if a day gets away from you, a quick follow-up in office hours or in your commonplace book picks the conversation back up.

If the spoken room is consistently hard for you, please talk to me early.  I'd much rather find a path that works for you than watch you quietly accept a grade you aren't happy with.

## Self-Assessment (Midterm and End of Term)

At midterm and again at the end of the semester, please write a short self-assessment (half a page is plenty).  Bring the midterm one to a quick conversation with me, so we can adjust while there is still time.  Submit the end-of-semester one through Canvas; that one earns this page's 10 points.  Please address:

1.  **Preparation:** How reliably have you been arriving with anchor passages and a question?  What is your evidence?
2.  **Contribution and listening:** Name one discussion you moved forward, and one classmate whose thinking changed yours.
3.  **Risk:** Name one moment you took an intellectual risk, such as taking a position you were unsure of or changing your mind in public.
4.  **A goal:** One concrete thing you will do differently in the second half of the term (or, at the end, one thing you will carry into your spring CIE section).

Your own account of your growth is what earns this page's points.  It also prevents surprises: if your sense of your participation and mine don't match, this is where we find that out and fix it.
